Reading Summary Output:

  • Min/Max: Range helps spot errors
  • Quartiles: Middle 50% of patients
  • Mean/Median: Similar = symmetric distribution
Understanding summary() Output
When you run summary() on a dataset, what does the "1st Qu." value represent?
The minimum value in the dataset
This would be the smallest value found in the data.
Not quite. The minimum value is shown as "Min." in the summary output. The 1st Qu. represents the first quartile.
The value below which 25% of the data falls
The first quartile divides the lower 25% from the upper 75%.
Correct! The 1st Qu. (first quartile) is the value below which 25% of the observations fall. It's a key measure in understanding data distribution.
The average of all values
The arithmetic mean of the dataset.
That's not right. The average (mean) is shown separately in the summary output as "Mean".
The most frequent value
The value that appears most often in the dataset.
Incorrect. The most frequent value is called the mode, which is not shown in the standard summary() output.

6.7.6. Correlation: Medical Detective Work

Does BMI correlate with blood pressure? Exercise with heart rate? Correlation reveals hidden connections driving health!

Correlation Scale:

  • 0.0-0.3: Weak
  • 0.3-0.7: Moderate
  • 0.7-1.0: Strong
Interpreting Correlation Values
If the correlation between medication dose and patient weight is 0.85, this indicates:
No relationship between dose and weight
The variables are independent of each other.
Incorrect. A correlation of 0.85 is quite high, indicating a strong relationship, not no relationship.
A weak positive relationship
There's a slight tendency for dose to increase with weight.
Not quite. 0.85 is well above the 0.7 threshold, making this a strong relationship, not weak.
A strong positive relationship
As weight increases, dose tends to increase substantially.
Correct! A correlation of 0.85 indicates a strong positive relationship. This makes clinical sense as heavier patients often require higher medication doses.
A perfect negative relationship
As weight increases, dose decreases perfectly.
Incorrect. The positive value (0.85) indicates a positive relationship, not negative. A perfect negative correlation would be -1.0.
# BMI and blood pressure relationship bmi_data <- c(22.5, 24.8, 28.3, 31.2, 26.5, 29.7, 33.5, 23.9, 27.1, 30.8, 25.4, 32.1, 28.9, 26.2, 34.2) systolic_bp <- c(118, 122, 135, 142, 128, 138, 155, 120, 130, 145, 125, 148, 136, 127, 160) bp_bmi_correlation <- cor(bmi_data, systolic_bp) cor_test <- cor.test(bmi_data, systolic_bp)

Comprehensive Understanding Check
A clinical researcher finds that the median glucose level (105 mg/dL) is higher than the mean (102 mg/dL) in a patient population. This suggests:
The data is normally distributed
Mean and median would be equal in a normal distribution.
Not correct. In a normal distribution, mean and median are typically very close or equal.
The data is left-skewed with some unusually low values
Low outliers pull the mean down below the median.
Correct! When median > mean, it indicates left skewness. Some unusually low glucose values are pulling the mean down while the median remains unaffected by outliers.
The data is right-skewed with some unusually high values
High outliers would pull the mean up above the median.
Incorrect. Right skewness would cause mean > median, not median > mean.
There is an error in the calculation
The median should never exceed the mean.
Wrong. It's perfectly normal for median to exceed mean in left-skewed distributions. This is not a calculation error.
