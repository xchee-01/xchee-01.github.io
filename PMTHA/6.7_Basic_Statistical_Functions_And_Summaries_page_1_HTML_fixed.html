Understanding Summary Statistics in Healthcare

Picture this: You're staring at blood pressure readings from 1,000 patients. 😵 Summary statistics instantly reveal the forest's shape, size, and health!

🏥 Real-world Scenario: When doctors say "your cholesterol is above normal," they're comparing your value to millions of data points condensed into simple statistics.

Quick Check
What is the primary purpose of summary statistics in healthcare?
To make data collection faster
This focuses on the process, not the purpose.
Not quite. Summary statistics don't speed up data collection - they help us understand data that's already been collected.
To transform large datasets into meaningful insights
Condensing complex data into understandable measures.
Exactly right! Summary statistics take thousands of data points and transform them into meaningful insights like "normal ranges" that doctors use every day.
To replace individual patient records
Statistics complement, not replace, individual data.
Incorrect. Summary statistics complement individual patient records but never replace them. Both are essential for comprehensive healthcare.
To eliminate the need for detailed analysis
Summary statistics are just the beginning of analysis.
Not correct. Summary statistics are often the starting point for deeper analysis, not a replacement for it.

Mean and Median: Your First Tools

# Blood pressure readings from 20 patients bp_systolic <- c(118, 125, 132, 140, 128, 155, 122, 138, 145, 130, 162, 127, 135, 148, 120, 142, 158, 133, 126, 151) # Calculate mean and median mean_bp <- mean(bp_systolic) median_bp <- median(bp_systolic) print(paste("Mean:", round(mean_bp, 1), "Median:", median_bp))
Copied!

🎯 Clinical Insight: The median (133.5) is lower than mean (136.8) because extreme values pull the mean upward!

Clinical Decision
A dataset of patient wait times shows: Mean = 45 minutes, Median = 30 minutes. What does this tell you?
The data is perfectly symmetrical
If symmetrical, mean and median would be equal.
Not correct. If the data were symmetrical, the mean and median would be approximately equal. The difference here indicates skewness.
Most patients wait exactly 45 minutes
The mean doesn't represent the most common value.
Incorrect. The mean doesn't tell us what "most" patients experience. With median at 30 minutes, half the patients wait less than 30 minutes.
There are some patients with very long wait times pulling the mean up
Outliers affect the mean more than the median.
Exactly! When the mean is higher than the median, it indicates right-skewed data with some very long wait times pulling the average up. This is common in healthcare wait times.
The median calculation is incorrect
This pattern is actually quite common and valid.
No, this is a perfectly valid and common pattern in healthcare data where outliers (very long waits) pull the mean higher than the median.

R Function Check
Which R function calculates the average of a numeric vector?
average()
This seems logical but isn't the R function name.
Not in R. While "average" is the everyday term, R uses a different function name for calculating the arithmetic mean.
mean()
The standard R function for calculating average.
Correct! In R, mean() calculates the arithmetic average. Remember: R uses specific function names that might differ from everyday language!
avg()
Common abbreviation but not used in base R.
No, R doesn't use avg(). The correct function is mean() for calculating the arithmetic average.
median()
This calculates the middle value, not the average.
median() calculates the middle value when data is sorted, not the average. The average is calculated using mean().

Measuring Variability: Consistency Matters!

Two medications both reduce BP by 10 points average. One works consistently (8-12), another unpredictably (0-20). Which would you prescribe? 🤔

# HbA1c values from diabetes patients hba1c <- c(6.2, 7.5, 8.1, 6.8, 9.2, 7.1, 8.5, 6.5, 7.8, 8.9, 6.9, 7.3, 8.3, 7.0, 9.5, 6.6, 7.7, 8.7, 7.2, 8.0) sd_hba1c <- sd(hba1c) iqr_hba1c <- IQR(hba1c)
Copied!

🎯 The 68-95-99.7 Rule:

  • 68% of patients fall within 1 SD
  • 95% within 2 SD
  • 99.7% within 3 SD
Statistical Rule Application
If a lab test has mean = 100 and SD = 10, approximately what percentage of healthy patients will have values between 80 and 120?
68%
This would be for one standard deviation (90-110).
Not quite. 68% covers ±1 SD, which would be 90-110 (mean ± 10). The range 80-120 is wider.
95%
Two standard deviations from the mean.
Excellent! 80 is 2 SDs below the mean (100-20) and 120 is 2 SDs above (100+20). The empirical rule tells us ~95% of values fall within ±2 SDs.
99.7%
This would be for three standard deviations (70-130).
Too high. 99.7% covers ±3 SD, which would be 70-130. The range 80-120 is only ±2 SD.
50%
This underestimates the coverage.
Too low. 50% would be roughly the interquartile range. The range 80-120 covers ±2 standard deviations.

Clinical Statistics
Which measure of variability is most appropriate when comparing datasets with different units or scales?
Standard deviation
This is in the same units as the original data.
Not ideal. Standard deviation is in the same units as the data, making it difficult to compare heart rate (beats/min) to blood pressure (mmHg).
Variance
This is in squared units, making comparison harder.
No. Variance is in squared units (e.g., beats²/min²), making cross-variable comparisons even more difficult than standard deviation.
Coefficient of variation
A unitless measure expressed as a percentage.
Correct! The coefficient of variation (CV = SD/mean × 100) is unitless, allowing fair comparison of variability between different measurements like heart rate and blood pressure.
Range
Simple but affected by outliers and has units.
Not suitable. Range (max-min) is still in the original units and is heavily influenced by outliers, making cross-variable comparisons unreliable.

Percentile Interpretation
A child's height is at the 15th percentile. What does this mean?
The child is 15% of average height
Percentiles don't represent fractions of average.
No. Percentiles don't represent a percentage of the average value. They show position relative to other children.
15% of children are taller than this child
You have the comparison backwards.
Backwards! The 15th percentile means 15% are shorter, not taller, than this child.
85% of children are taller than this child
15th percentile means 85% are above this value.
Correct! At the 15th percentile, 15% of children are shorter and 85% are taller. Percentiles show position relative to a reference population.
The child is 15 inches tall
Percentiles are not measurements.
No. Percentiles indicate relative position in a distribution, not actual measurements. The 15th percentile is a ranking, not a height.

💭 Clinical Thinking: Children below 5th percentile may need growth hormone evaluation!

Answer:

heights <- c(132, 138, 145, 129, 141, 135, 143, 130, 147, 134, 140, 128, 142, 136, 144, 131, 139, 146, 133, 137, 148, 127, 135, 141, 130, 143, 138, 134, 140, 145) # Find clinical concern thresholds concern_percentiles <- quantile(heights, probs = c(0.05, 0.95)) below_5th <- sum(heights < concern_percentiles[1])
Copied!
R Syntax
To find the 5th and 95th percentiles in R, which syntax is correct?
quantile(data, c(5, 95))
This would look for the 5th and 95th values.
Not correct. Without the probs parameter, R interprets these as positions, not percentiles.
quantile(data, probs = c(0.05, 0.95))
Correct syntax using probabilities.
Perfect! R's quantile() function expects probabilities (0 to 1), not percentages. So 5th percentile = 0.05, 95th percentile = 0.95.
percentile(data, c(5, 95))
This function doesn't exist in base R.
No such function in base R. The correct function is quantile(), not percentile().
quantile(data, probs = c(5%, 95%))
R doesn't recognize the % symbol this way.
Syntax error! R doesn't recognize % in numeric contexts like this. Use decimal probabilities: 0.05 and 0.95.

Final Check
You're analyzing patient recovery times after surgery. The data shows: Mean = 14 days, Median = 10 days, SD = 8 days. Which statement is most likely true?
All patients recover in exactly 14 days
The SD of 8 days indicates variability.
No. A standard deviation of 8 days shows significant variability in recovery times. If all recovered in 14 days, SD would be 0.
The data is left-skewed with most patients recovering quickly
Consider the mean-median relationship.
Incorrect. When mean > median, the data is right-skewed, not left-skewed. The higher mean suggests longer recovery times pulling the average up.
The data is right-skewed with some patients taking much longer to recover
Mean > median indicates right skew.
Exactly right! Mean (14) > Median (10) indicates right skewness. Most patients recover relatively quickly (around 10 days), but some take much longer, pulling the mean higher.
The standard deviation calculation must be wrong
This SD is reasonable for this scenario.
The SD of 8 days is perfectly reasonable for recovery time data with mean 14 days. It indicates substantial but realistic variability in patient outcomes.
