1. Basic Structure of Amino Acids

General amino acid structure showing central alpha carbon with four groups attached.

  • General Formula: H2N-CαHR-COOH
  • Central Carbon (Cα): Also called the α-carbon
    • Tetrahedral structure
    • Forms the backbone of all amino acids
    • Connects four different groups:
      1. Amino group (-NH2)
      2. Carboxyl group (-COOH)
      3. Hydrogen atom (-H)
      4. Side chain (-R)

1.1. Types of Carbons in Amino Acids

  • Alpha-carbon (Cα): The central carbon to which the amino group, carboxyl group, hydrogen, and side chain are attached
  • Beta-carbon (Cβ): The first carbon in the side chain, directly attached to Cα
  • Gamma-carbon (Cγ): The second carbon in the side chain
  • Delta-carbon (Cδ): The third carbon in the side chain
  • Epsilon-carbon (Cε): The fourth carbon in the side chain

1.2. Stereochemistry of Amino Acids

  • Chiral Center: The α-carbon is a chiral center in all amino acids except glycine
    • Four different groups attached to Cα create asymmetry
    • Glycine has two hydrogens, making it achiral
  • Enantiomers: Two possible spatial arrangements around the α-carbon
    • D-configuration (dextrorotatory)
    • L-configuration (levorotatory)
  • L-Configuration in Biological Systems:
    • Almost all naturally occurring amino acids in proteins are L-amino acids
    • Defined by the Fischer projection
    • In L-amino acids, the α-carbon has:
      • Amino group (-NH2) on the left
      • Carboxyl group (-COOH) at the top
      • Side chain (-R) on the right
      • Hydrogen atom (-H) at the bottom
  • Absolute Configuration: Can also be described using R/S system
    • Most L-amino acids have an S configuration at the α-carbon
    • Exception: L-cysteine has an R configuration due to priority rules

2. Zwitterionic Form

2.1. Definition and Formation

  • Definition: A zwitterion is a molecule with both positive and negative charges
  • Formation:
    • At physiological pH, the amino group is protonated (-NH₃⁺)
    • The carboxyl group is deprotonated (-COO⁻)
    • This creates a dipolar ion or zwitterion

2.2. Charge Distribution

  • α-amino group: positively charged at pH < 9
  • α-carboxyl group: negatively charged at pH > 2
  • Side chain: may be charged depending on its pKa and the pH

2.3. Significance

  • Contributes to amino acid solubility in water
  • Affects interactions with other molecules
  • Influences protein structure and function

Note: The zwitterionic nature of amino acids explains why they can behave as both acids and bases, and contributes to their unique chemical properties.

3. Titration Curves

3.1. Definition and Features

  • Definition: A plot showing how pH changes as acid or base is added to a solution of an amino acid
  • Features of amino acid titration curves:
    • Multiple inflection points corresponding to each ionizable group
    • Plateaus (buffer regions) around each pKa value
    • The number of inflection points depends on the number of ionizable groups

3.2. Simple Amino Acid Titration (e.g., Glycine)

  • Three regions:
    • Low pH: both α-COOH and α-NH₃⁺ are protonated (net charge: +1)
    • Middle pH: α-COOH deprotonated, α-NH₃⁺ protonated (net charge: 0)
    • High pH: both α-COOH and α-NH₃⁺ deprotonated (net charge: -1)
  • Two inflection points:
    • First inflection: pKa of α-COOH (~2.34)
    • Second inflection: pKa of α-NH₃⁺ (~9.60)

3.3. Amino Acids with Ionizable Side Chains

  • Additional inflection point corresponding to the side chain
  • Example: Histidine
    • First inflection: pKa of α-COOH (~1.82)
    • Second inflection: pKa of imidazole side chain (~6.0)
    • Third inflection: pKa of α-NH₃⁺ (~9.17)

4. Isoelectric Point (pI)

4.1. Definition

The pH at which the amino acid carries no net charge

4.2. Calculation

  • For amino acids with no ionizable side chains: pI = (pKa₁ + pKa₂)/2
  • For amino acids with ionizable side chains: depends on the relative pKa values

4.3. Significance

  • At pI, amino acids have minimum solubility in water
  • Basis for isoelectric focusing in protein purification
  • Proteins precipitate at their pI

Note: The isoelectric point is a crucial parameter in protein separation techniques like electrophoresis and chromatography, as it determines the pH at which a protein will not migrate in an electric field.

5. pKa Values of Amino Acid Functional Groups

5.1. α-Carboxyl Groups

  • Typical pKa range: 1.8 - 2.4
  • Generally more acidic than isolated carboxylic acids (pKa ~4.5)
  • Reason for increased acidity: The adjacent positively charged α-NH₃⁺ group stabilizes the negative charge of the carboxylate
  • Variation among amino acids: Small differences due to side chain effects

5.2. α-Amino Groups

  • Typical pKa range: 8.8 - 10.8
  • Generally less basic than isolated amines (pKa ~10.5)
  • Reason for decreased basicity: The adjacent negatively charged α-COO⁻ destabilizes the positive charge on the ammonium
  • Variation among amino acids: Influenced by proximity and nature of side chains

5.3. Side Chain Functional Groups

5.3.1. Acidic Side Chains

  • Aspartic acid (Asp, D):
    • Side chain: carboxylic acid (-COOH)
    • pKa: ~3.9 (more acidic than α-COOH)
    • Charged at physiological pH: Yes, negative (-COO⁻)
  • Glutamic acid (Glu, E):
    • Side chain: carboxylic acid (-COOH)
    • pKa: ~4.1 (more acidic than α-COOH)
    • Charged at physiological pH: Yes, negative (-COO⁻)

5.3.2. Basic Side Chains

  • Lysine (Lys, K):
    • Side chain: ε-amino group (-NH₂)
    • pKa: ~10.5
    • Charged at physiological pH: Yes, positive (-NH₃⁺)
  • Arginine (Arg, R):
    • Side chain: guanidino group
    • pKa: ~12.5 (very basic)
    • Charged at physiological pH: Yes, positive
  • Histidine (His, H):
    • Side chain: imidazole group
    • pKa: ~6.0 (near physiological pH)
    • Charged at physiological pH: Partially (exists in both protonated and deprotonated forms)
    • Special significance: Acts as both acid and base in enzyme catalysis

5.3.3. Polar Side Chains (Weakly Ionizable)

  • Cysteine (Cys, C):
    • Side chain: thiol (-SH)
    • pKa: ~8.3
    • Charged at physiological pH: Mostly no, but some thiolate (-S⁻) exists
  • Tyrosine (Tyr, Y):
    • Side chain: phenolic hydroxyl (-OH)
    • pKa: ~10.1
    • Charged at physiological pH: No (predominantly protonated)
  • Serine (Ser, S) and Threonine (Thr, T):
    • Side chain: hydroxyl (-OH)
    • pKa: ~13-16 (very weakly acidic)
    • Charged at physiological pH: No

6. Factors Affecting pKa Values

6.1. Microenvironment Effects

6.1.1. Electrostatic Interactions

  • Proximity to charged groups:
    • Positively charged groups decrease pKa (increase acidity)
    • Negatively charged groups increase pKa (decrease acidity)
  • Example: Asp residue near a positive charge may have a pKa of 6-7 instead of the standard ~4

6.1.2. Hydrogen Bonding

  • Donation of H-bonds:
    • Stabilizes the conjugate base
    • Decreases pKa (increases acidity)
  • Acceptance of H-bonds:
    • Stabilizes the protonated form
    • Increases pKa (decreases acidity)
  • Example: Ser/Thr hydroxyls can form H-bonds with carboxylates, raising their pKa

6.1.3. Dielectric Constant

  • Definition: Measure of a medium's ability to reduce the force between charged particles
  • Water: High dielectric constant (ε ~80) that shields charges
  • Protein interior: Low dielectric constant (ε ~2-4)
  • Effect:
    • Low dielectric environments increase the strength of electrostatic interactions
    • Charged groups are destabilized in hydrophobic environments
    • Acidic groups tend to have higher pKa values in the protein interior
    • Basic groups tend to have lower pKa values in the protein interior

6.2. Protein Folding and pKa Shifts

6.2.1. Burial of Ionizable Groups

  • Hydrophobic core: Unfavorable for charged species due to desolvation penalty
  • Consequence:
    • Buried acidic residues (Asp, Glu) have increased pKa values (less acidic)
    • Buried basic residues (Lys, Arg) have decreased pKa values (less basic)
  • Example: A buried Asp can have a pKa as high as 7-9 compared to the standard ~4

6.2.2. Ion Pairs (Salt Bridges)

  • Definition: Electrostatic interaction between oppositely charged groups
  • Effect on pKa:
    • Stabilizes charged forms of both partners
    • Can increase pKa of bases and decrease pKa of acids
  • Importance:
    • Contributes to protein stability
    • Can be critical for function

6.2.3. Conformational Changes

  • Dynamic nature of proteins:
    • pKa values can change during conformational shifts
    • Local environment around ionizable groups alters during protein motion
  • Allosteric regulation:
    • Binding events can cause pKa shifts at distant sites
    • Can trigger protonation/deprotonation events

6.3. Solvent Accessibility

6.3.1. Exposed vs. Buried Residues

  • Exposed residues:
    • Interact with water
    • pKa values closer to standard values
  • Partially buried residues:
    • Limited solvent access
    • Intermediate pKa shifts
  • Fully buried residues:
    • Maximum pKa perturbation
    • May be involved in specialized functions

6.3.2. Hydration Effects

  • Hydration shells:
    • Water molecules organize around charged groups
    • Stabilize ionic forms
  • Dehydration penalty:
    • Energetic cost of removing water from charged groups
    • Major contributor to pKa shifts in proteins

6.3.3. Quantitative Relationship

  • Born equation describes the electrostatic contribution to pKa shifts:
ΔpKa = (166 × z²/r) × (1/ε_protein - 1/ε_water)
  • Where:
    • z is the charge
    • r is the radius (Å)
    • ε is the dielectric constant

7. pKa in Protein Structure and Function

7.1. Role in Protein Stability

7.1.1. Electrostatic Interactions

  • Salt bridges:
    • Form between oppositely charged side chains
    • Contribute ~3-5 kcal/mol to stability
    • Highly dependent on pKa values and local pH
  • Charge-dipole interactions:
    • Between charged groups and polar residues
    • Weaker than salt bridges but more numerous

7.1.2. pH-Dependent Stability

  • Protein denaturation at extreme pH:
    • High pH: deprotonation of all groups leads to excess negative charge
    • Low pH: protonation of all groups leads to excess positive charge
    • Both cause electrostatic repulsion and unfolding
  • Stability maximum:
    • Often near the protein's isoelectric point
    • Minimizes net charge and repulsive forces

7.1.3. Thermodynamic Coupling

  • Protonation and conformation:
    • Linked equilibria
    • Changes in one affect the other
  • Proton-linked folding:
    • Uptake or release of protons during folding/unfolding
    • Provides information about pKa changes

7.2. Active Site Catalysis

7.2.1. Catalytic Triads and Dyads

  • Serine proteases (trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase):
    • His-Asp-Ser catalytic triad
    • pKa of His elevated to ~7 (from standard ~6)
    • Enables proton shuttling during catalysis
  • Cysteine proteases (papain, cathepsins):
    • His-Cys catalytic dyad
    • pKa of Cys lowered to ~4 (from standard ~8.3)
    • Increases nucleophilicity

7.2.2. Acid-Base Catalysis

  • General acid catalysis:
    • Protonation of leaving groups
    • Enhanced by residues with pKa near operating pH
  • General base catalysis:
    • Deprotonation of nucleophiles
    • Enhanced by residues with pKa near operating pH
  • Concerted acid-base catalysis:
    • Simultaneous proton donation and acceptance
    • Requires precise positioning of catalytic residues

7.2.3. pKa Modulation in Enzymes

  • Perturbed pKa values:
    • Often essential for catalytic function
    • Can differ dramatically from standard values
  • Examples:
    • Ribonuclease A: Two catalytic His residues with pKa values of ~6 and ~8
    • Lysozyme: Glu35 with elevated pKa (~6.5) acts as a general acid
    • Carbonic anhydrase: Zn²⁺-bound water with pKa ~7 acts as a nucleophile

7.3. pH-Dependent Conformational Changes

7.3.1. pH Sensors in Proteins

  • Histidine switches:
    • pKa (~6) near physiological pH
    • Acts as biological pH sensor
    • Example: Histidine kinases in bacterial two-component systems

7.3.2. Allosteric Regulation

  • Hemoglobin:
    • Bohr effect: pH affects oxygen binding affinity
    • Low pH promotes oxygen release in tissues
    • His146 (pKa ~7.1) forms salt bridge upon protonation

7.3.3. Membrane Protein Function

  • Proton channels and pumps:
    • Use pKa shifts to transport protons
    • Example: Bacteriorhodopsin uses Asp96 and Asp85 as proton acceptors/donors
  • pH-gated channels:
    • Open or close in response to pH changes
    • Critical for acid sensing in neurons

7.3.4. Protein-Protein Interactions

  • pH-dependent binding:
    • Ionization state affects interface complementarity
    • Can act as regulatory mechanism
  • Example: Calmodulin-target interactions often involve His residues

Note: Understanding the pKa values of amino acids and how they are affected by the protein environment is crucial for predicting protein behavior, designing enzymes, and developing pH-responsive biomaterials.
