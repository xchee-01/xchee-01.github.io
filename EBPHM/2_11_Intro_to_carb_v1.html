1. Introduction to Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates represent one of the four major classes of biomolecules alongside proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. They are the most abundant organic compounds found in nature and serve as the primary source of energy for most organisms. The term "carbohydrate" derives from their chemical composition as hydrates of carbon, with the general formula Cx(H2O)y, although many carbohydrates deviate from this precise formula.

Note: Carbohydrates are essential for life, serving not only as energy sources but also as structural components and playing crucial roles in cell recognition and signaling.

2. Classification of Carbohydrates

2.1. Based on Size and Complexity

  1. Monosaccharides: Simple sugars that cannot be hydrolyzed into smaller carbohydrate units
    • Common examples: glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose, deoxyribose
    • Usually contain 3-7 carbon atoms (trioses, tetroses, pentoses, hexoses, heptoses)
  2. Oligosaccharides: Composed of 2-10 monosaccharide units joined by glycosidic bonds
    • Disaccharides (2 units): sucrose, lactose, maltose, trehalose
    • Trisaccharides (3 units): raffinose, maltotriose
    • Tetrasaccharides (4 units): stachyose

2.2. Based on Reducing Properties

  1. Reducing sugars: Possess a free hemiacetal or hemiketal group that can act as a reducing agent
    • All monosaccharides
    • Some disaccharides (maltose, lactose, cellobiose)
  2. Non-reducing sugars: Lack a free hemiacetal or hemiketal group
    • Examples: sucrose, trehalose

3. Monosaccharides: Structure and Properties

3.1. Stereochemistry

  1. Chirality: Most monosaccharides contain multiple chiral centers
    • D and L configuration: determined by the configuration of the highest-numbered chiral carbon
    • D-sugars predominate in nature
  2. Epimers: Monosaccharides that differ in configuration at only one carbon atom
    • Glucose and galactose are C-4 epimers
    • Glucose and mannose are C-2 epimers
  3. Anomeric carbon: The carbonyl carbon in the cyclic form of a monosaccharide
    • Forms α and β anomers depending on the orientation of the hydroxyl group

3.2. Cyclic Structures

  1. Hemiacetal/hemiketal formation: Intramolecular reaction between the carbonyl group and a hydroxyl group
    • Furanose ring: 5-membered ring (e.g., ribose in RNA)
    • Pyranose ring: 6-membered ring (e.g., glucose in most biological contexts)
  2. Conformational analysis:
    • Chair, boat, and twist-boat conformations in pyranoses
    • Envelope and twist conformations in furanoses
    • Most hexoses adopt the chair conformation due to its stability

3.3. Types of Monosaccharides Based on Carbon Count

  1. Trioses (3 carbons):
    • Glyceraldehyde: Important in glycolysis and gluconeogenesis
    • Dihydroxyacetone: A ketose involved in glycolysis
  2. Tetroses (4 carbons):
    • Erythrose: Involved in the pentose phosphate pathway
    • Threose: Precursor to threose nucleic acid (TNA)
  3. Pentoses (5 carbons):
    • Ribose: Component of RNA and several coenzymes (ATP, NAD, FAD)
    • Deoxyribose: Component of DNA
    • Xylose: Found in plant cell walls
    • Arabinose: Component of plant glycoproteins and cell walls
  4. Hexoses (6 carbons):
    • Glucose: Primary energy source for most organisms
    • Galactose: Component of lactose and many glycolipids
    • Mannose: Component of many glycoproteins
    • Fructose: Found in fruits and honey, component of sucrose
  5. Heptoses (7 carbons):
    • Sedoheptulose: Intermediate in the pentose phosphate pathway
    • Mannoheptulose: Found in avocados

Key Takeaway: Understanding monosaccharide structure and stereochemistry is fundamental to comprehending the more complex carbohydrates and their biological functions. These simple sugars serve as building blocks for more complex carbohydrates and play critical roles in metabolism and energy production.
