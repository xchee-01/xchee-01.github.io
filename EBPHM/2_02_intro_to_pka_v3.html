1. Basic Concepts of Acids and Bases

1.1. Brønsted-Lowry Definition

  • Acid: A species that donates a proton (H⁺)
  • Base: A species that accepts a proton (H⁺)
  • Conjugate acid-base pair: When an acid donates a proton, it becomes its conjugate base; when a base accepts a proton, it becomes its conjugate acid

1.2. Acid-Base Equilibria

  • Acid-base reactions establish dynamic equilibria:
HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻
  • The equilibrium constant (Ka) for acid dissociation is:
Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]
  • The stronger the acid, the larger the Ka value
  • pKa is defined as the negative logarithm of Ka:
pKa = -log₁₀(Ka)
  • The stronger the acid, the smaller the pKa value
  • Typical pKa range in biochemistry: -2 to 14

1.3. Relationship Between pH and pKa

  • pH is defined as the negative logarithm of hydrogen ion concentration:
pH = -log₁₀[H⁺]
  • At equilibrium: When pH = pKa, [HA] = [A⁻] (equal concentrations of acid and conjugate base)
  • When pH < pKa, the acid form predominates
  • When pH > pKa, the conjugate base form predominates

2. The Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation

2.1. Derivation of the Equation

Starting with the acid dissociation constant:

Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]

Taking the negative log of both sides:

-log(Ka) = -log([H⁺][A⁻]/[HA])
pKa = -log[H⁺] - log([A⁻]/[HA])
pKa = pH - log([A⁻]/[HA])

Rearranging:

pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA])

This is the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation.

2.2. Applications of the Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation

2.2.1. Calculating pH of Buffer Solutions

  • When a weak acid (HA) and its conjugate base (A⁻) are present in solution, they form a buffer
  • The pH of the buffer can be calculated using:
pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA])
  • Buffer capacity is highest when [A⁻] ≈ [HA], which occurs at pH ≈ pKa

2.2.2. Predicting Ionization States

  • Allows calculation of the ratio of deprotonated to protonated species at any pH
  • At pH = pKa: log([A⁻]/[HA]) = 0, so [A⁻]/[HA] = 1 (equal amounts)
  • At pH = pKa + 1: [A⁻]/[HA] = 10 (90.9% deprotonated)
  • At pH = pKa - 1: [A⁻]/[HA] = 0.1 (9.1% deprotonated)
  • At pH = pKa + 2: [A⁻]/[HA] = 100 (99% deprotonated)
  • At pH = pKa - 2: [A⁻]/[HA] = 0.01 (1% deprotonated)

2.3. The Rule of Thumb

  • When pH is within ±1 unit of pKa, significant amounts of both protonated and deprotonated forms exist
  • When pH is more than 2 units above pKa, the species is >99% deprotonated
  • When pH is more than 2 units below pKa, the species is >99% protonated

3. Importance of pKa in Biochemistry

3.1. Protein Structure and Function

  • pKa values determine the ionization state of amino acid side chains
  • Ionization states affect:
    • Protein folding and stability
    • Electrostatic interactions
    • Hydrogen bonding
    • Solubility
    • Binding of ligands
    • Catalytic activity

3.2. Enzyme Catalysis

  • Many enzyme reactions involve acid-base catalysis
  • Catalytic amino acids must be in the correct protonation state
  • pKa values of active site residues are often perturbed from their standard values
  • Histidine (pKa ~6.0) is often involved in catalysis because its pKa is close to physiological pH

3.3. Physiological pH Regulation

  • Blood pH is tightly regulated (pH ~7.4)
  • Intracellular pH varies by organelle (pH ~5.0-7.2)
  • Understanding pKa helps predict which amino acids will be charged in different cellular compartments

3.4. Drug Design and Pharmacokinetics

  • pKa affects drug:
    • Solubility
    • Membrane permeability
    • Binding to protein targets
    • Absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion (ADME)
