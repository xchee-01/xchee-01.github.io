Slide 1 - Time: 0:01:16

Basic Structure of Amino Acids

Question 1: What type of reaction joins amino acids together during protein synthesis?

A) Oxidation reaction
B) Condensation reaction
C) Substitution reaction
D) Hydrolysis reaction

Explanation: As shown in slide 1, amino acids are joined together through peptide bond formation, which is specifically described as a "condensation reaction" in protein synthesis. During this process, the carboxyl group (-COOH) of one amino acid reacts with the amino group (-NH2) of another, releasing a water molecule and creating a covalent C-N bond known as a peptide bond.

Question 2: What is the general formula of an amino acid?

A) H-CαHR-COOH
B) H2N-CHR-COOH
C) H2N-CαHR-COOH
D) H2N-CαHR-CO

Explanation: As stated in slide 1, amino acids have the general formula of H2N-CαHR-COOH, which includes an amino group (H2N-), an alpha carbon with a hydrogen and R-group (CαHR), and a carboxyl group (-COOH).

Slide 2 - Time: 0:01:35

Types of Carbons in Amino Acids

Question 1: What are the four substituents attached to the α carbon in an amino acid?

A) Carboxyl group, R group, two hydrogen atoms
B) Amino group, carboxyl group, hydrogen, R group
C) Amino group, hydroxyl group, hydrogen, R group
D) Carboxyl group, amine group, two R groups

Explanation: As detailed in slide 2, the α carbon in an amino acid always has four substituents in a tetrahedral arrangement: an acidic carboxyl group (-COOH), a basic amino group (-NH2), an α hydrogen, and a fourth substituent called an "R group" which varies between different amino acids.

Question 2: What distinguishes α-amino acids from β- and γ-amino acids?

A) The position of the carboxyl group
B) The type of R group attached
C) The position of the amino group relative to the carboxyl group
D) The number of carbon atoms in the molecule

Explanation: The slide shows examples of α-glycine, β-alanine, and γ-amino acid, illustrating that these classifications refer to the position of the amino group in relation to the carboxyl group. In α-amino acids, the amino group is attached to the carbon adjacent to the carboxyl group (the α-carbon). In β-amino acids, it's attached to the β-carbon (one carbon further away), and in γ-amino acids, it's attached to the γ-carbon (two carbons away from the carboxyl group).

Slide 3 - Time: 0:03:33

Stereochemistry of Amino Acids

Question 1: Why is glycine not considered a chiral amino acid?

A) Because it lacks a carboxyl group
B) Because it has two amino groups
C) Because its R group is a hydrogen atom, making two identical substituents on the α-carbon
D) Because it only exists in D-configuration

Explanation: The slide mentions that in most amino acids, the α-carbon serves as a chiral center, creating molecular asymmetry through the attachment of four distinct groups "except glycine." Glycine's R group is a hydrogen atom, which means the α-carbon has two hydrogen atoms attached (the R group hydrogen and the α-hydrogen). Since chirality requires four different substituents, glycine with two identical hydrogens is not chiral.

Question 2: Which configuration of amino acids predominates in naturally occurring proteins?

A) D-configuration
B) L-configuration
C) Equal mixture of D and L configurations
D) Neither D nor L configuration

Explanation: According to slide 3, "Biological systems overwhelmingly favor the L-configuration, with nearly all naturally occurring proteins composed exclusively of L-amino acids." This stereospecificity is a fundamental characteristic of biological systems.
