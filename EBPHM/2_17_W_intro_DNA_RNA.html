1. Nucleic Acids: The Information Molecules

1.1. Historical Context

  • 1869: Friedrich Miescher first isolated "nuclein" from cell nuclei
  • 1944: Avery-MacLeod-McCarty experiment demonstrated DNA as the genetic material
  • 1952: Hershey-Chase experiment confirmed DNA as the genetic material using bacteriophages
  • 1953: Watson and Crick proposed the double helix structure of DNA based on Rosalind Franklin's X-ray crystallography data

1.2. Fundamental Roles

  • DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid): Long-term storage of genetic information
  • RNA (Ribonucleic Acid): Multiple roles including genetic information transmission, catalysis, and regulation
  • Both function as informational polymers that direct protein synthesis and cellular function

2. Chemical Components of Nucleic Acids

2.1. Nucleotide Structure

  • Nucleotides: The basic building blocks of nucleic acids
  • Three components:
    • Nitrogenous base: Heterocyclic compound containing nitrogen
    • Pentose sugar: 5-carbon sugar (ribose in RNA, deoxyribose in DNA)
    • Phosphate group: Derived from phosphoric acid

2.2. Nitrogenous Bases

  • Purines: Double-ring structures
    • Adenine (A): Present in both DNA and RNA
    • Guanine (G): Present in both DNA and RNA
  • Pyrimidines: Single-ring structures
    • Cytosine (C): Present in both DNA and RNA
    • Thymine (T): Present only in DNA
    • Uracil (U): Present only in RNA (replaces thymine)

2.3. Pentose Sugars

  • Deoxyribose (in DNA): Lacks an oxygen atom at the 2' carbon position
  • Ribose (in RNA): Contains a hydroxyl (-OH) group at the 2' carbon position
  • This structural difference significantly affects stability and reactivity

2.4. Phosphate Groups

  • Connect nucleotides through phosphodiester bonds
  • Create the sugar-phosphate backbone of nucleic acids
  • Provide negative charge to nucleic acids at physiological pH

3. DNA Structure in Detail

3.1. Primary Structure

  • Linear sequence of nucleotides
  • Directionality: 5' to 3' (refers to carbon positions in the sugar)
  • Phosphodiester bonds: Connect the 3' carbon of one nucleotide to the 5' carbon of the next
  • Complementary base pairing: A pairs with T, G pairs with C

3.2. Secondary Structure (B-DNA)

  • Double helix: Two polynucleotide strands coiled around a common axis
  • Antiparallel arrangement: Strands run in opposite directions (5'→3' and 3'→5')
  • Right-handed helix: Most common form in cells
  • Dimensions:
    • Diameter: 2.0 nm
    • Complete turn: 10.5 base pairs
    • Rise per base pair: 0.34 nm
    • Distance per complete turn: 3.57 nm

3.3. Hydrogen Bonding

  • A-T pairs: Two hydrogen bonds
  • G-C pairs: Three hydrogen bonds
  • Implications: G-C rich regions have higher thermal stability

3.4. Major and Minor Grooves

  • Major groove: Wider, deeper channel
    • More accessible to proteins
    • Contains more chemical information for protein recognition
  • Minor groove: Narrower, shallower channel
    • Less accessible but still important for some DNA-binding proteins

3.5. Alternative DNA Structures

  • A-DNA: Dehydrated form, wider and flatter than B-DNA
  • Z-DNA: Left-handed helix, zigzag pattern of the phosphate backbone
  • Cruciform structures: Form at palindromic sequences
  • G-quadruplexes: Four-stranded structures formed in G-rich regions
  • Triple helix: Three strands interacting through Hoogsteen hydrogen bonding

4. RNA Structure in Detail

4.1. Chemical Distinctions from DNA

  • Contains ribose sugar with 2'-OH group (absent in DNA)
  • Uses uracil instead of thymine
  • Usually single-stranded, allowing for complex folding

4.2. Primary Structure

  • Linear sequence of nucleotides
  • Same directionality (5' to 3') and phosphodiester bond chemistry as DNA
  • Usually exists as a single strand

4.3. Secondary Structure

  • Self-complementary regions form through intramolecular base pairing
  • Common structural motifs:
    • Stem-loops (hairpins): Double-stranded region with single-stranded loop
    • Bulges: Unpaired nucleotides within a double-stranded region
    • Internal loops: Unpaired nucleotides on both strands
    • Junctions: Where multiple helical segments meet
    • Pseudoknots: Non-nested base-pairing arrangements

4.4. Tertiary Structure

  • Three-dimensional folding of the entire RNA molecule
  • Stabilized by:
    • Base stacking interactions
    • Metal ion coordination (especially Mg²⁺)
    • Long-range hydrogen bonding
    • Base triplets and quartets
    • Ribose zippers (hydrogen bonding between 2'-OH groups)

4.5. Major Types of RNA

  • Messenger RNA (mRNA): Carries protein-coding information from DNA
  • Transfer RNA (tRNA): Transports amino acids during protein synthesis
    • Distinctive cloverleaf secondary structure
    • L-shaped tertiary structure
  • Ribosomal RNA (rRNA): Structural and catalytic component of ribosomes
  • Non-coding RNAs: Regulatory roles (miRNA, siRNA, lncRNA, etc.)

5. Stabilizing Forces in Nucleic Acid Structures

5.1. Hydrogen Bonding

  • Primary force for complementary base pairing
  • Provides specificity for correct base pairing
  • Relatively weak individually but collectively significant

5.2. Base Stacking Interactions

  • Hydrophobic and van der Waals interactions between adjacent bases
  • Major contributor to double helix stability
  • More significant energetically than hydrogen bonding

5.3. Hydration

  • Ordered water molecules in grooves stabilize structure
  • Water bridges between phosphates and bases
  • Essential for maintaining B-form DNA

5.4. Electrostatic Interactions

  • Negatively charged phosphate groups repel each other
  • Neutralized by positively charged ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺)
  • Critical for proper folding of RNA molecules

6. Key Takeaways and Study Points

  1. DNA and RNA are polymers of nucleotides, each containing a phosphate group, a pentose sugar, and a nitrogenous base.
  2. Key structural differences between DNA and RNA:
    • DNA: Deoxyribose sugar, thymine base, typically double-stranded
    • RNA: Ribose sugar, uracil base, typically single-stranded with complex folding
  3. DNA primarily exists as a right-handed double helix with complementary base pairing (A-T, G-C), while RNA forms diverse secondary and tertiary structures through intramolecular interactions.
  4. The structure of nucleic acids is stabilized by hydrogen bonding, base stacking, hydration, and electrostatic interactions.
  5. Nucleic acid structure directly influences biological function, including replication, transcription, translation, and regulation.
  6. Understanding nucleic acid structure is essential for advances in medicine, biotechnology, and synthetic biology.

Note: This overview provides the essential structural details of DNA and RNA. Understanding these structural aspects is crucial for comprehending how genetic information is stored, transferred, and expressed in biological systems.
